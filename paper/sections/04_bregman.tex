\section{Bregman divergences for value learning}
\label{sec:04_bregman}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Any Bregman divergence learns the value function}

For a strictly convex, differentiable potential $\varphi$ on $(0,\infty)$, the
Bregman divergence \citep{bregman1967relaxation} of a prediction $u$ from a
target $T$ is
\begin{equation}
  \Breg(T, u) = \varphi(T) - \varphi(u) - \varphi'(u)\,(T - u).
  \label{eq:bregman-def}
\end{equation}
Squared error is the special case $\varphi(u)=u^{2}$, giving
$\Breg(T,u)=(T-u)^{2}$. The order of the arguments matters: the prediction must
occupy the \emph{second} slot, which is the one whose minimizer is a plain
mean.\footnote{With the arguments swapped, $\arg\min_u\E[\Breg(u,T)]$ solves
$\varphi'(u)=\E[\varphi'(T)]$, a quasi-arithmetic mean --- the harmonic mean of
$T$ for $\varphi=-\ln u$, not $\E[T]$.}

The fact that a Bregman divergence is minimised in expectation by the mean is
classical --- it is the Savage representation of proper scoring rules
\citep{savage1971elicitation,gneiting2007proper}, and Bregman divergences are
exactly the losses with that property \citep{banerjee2005clustering}. What we
add is the reparameterisation: because the value is a \emph{log}-partition
function, the network predicts $p=\log u$, and in that variable the whole
family collapses onto a single scalar weight.

\begin{lemma}[Gradient weight; the minimiser is classical]
\label{lem:minimizer}
Let the regression target be $e^{q}$, with $q$ such that
$\E[e^{q}\mid x_t]=e^{\Vf(x_t)}$ (the H-martingale target,
\S\ref{sec:02_setting}). Parameterize the prediction in log space, $u=e^{p}$.
Then for any strictly convex $\varphi$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial p}\,\Breg\big(e^{q},\,e^{p}\big)
  \;=\; w(e^{p})\,\big(e^{p}-e^{q}\big),
  \qquad
  w(u) \;:=\; u\,\varphi''(u) \;>\; 0,
  \label{eq:weight-grad}
\end{equation}
and consequently
$\arg\min_{p}\ \E_{q\mid x_t}\!\big[\Breg(e^{q},e^{p})\big] = \Vf(x_t)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Differentiating \eqref{eq:bregman-def} in $u$, the $\varphi'(u)$ terms cancel
and leave $\varphi''(u)(u-T)$; the chain rule contributes
$\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}p = u$. Because \eqref{eq:weight-grad} is \emph{linear}
in the target, taking expectations gives
$\E[\partial L/\partial p] = w(e^{p})\,(e^{p}-\E[e^{q}\mid x_t])$, which is
negative below $e^{\Vf}$ and positive above it. Full proof in
App.~\ref{sec:D_proofs}.
\end{proof}

\noindent Two consequences drive the rest of this section. First, the choice of
$\varphi$ does \emph{not} bias the learned value, so it is free to be chosen for
\emph{conditioning}. Second, \eqref{eq:weight-grad} says that a Bregman loss
acts on the optimizer only through the scalar \emph{gradient weight} $w$
multiplying the raw exp-space residual $e^{p}-e^{q}$. \textbf{The design space
is exactly the choice of a positive weight function $w$ on $(0,\infty)$}, and
the two losses we have met are its opposite extremes.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Two reference points}

\paragraph{Squared error: $w(u)=2u$.}
The weight \emph{grows} with the prediction, so
$\partial L/\partial p = 2e^{p}(e^{p}-e^{q})$ explodes like $e^{2p}$ for
over-predictions and, because $w(u)\to0$ as $u\to0$, is driven to zero for
under-predictions --- the unrecoverable pathology of \S\ref{sec:03_expmse}.

\paragraph{Itakura--Saito: $w(u)=1/u$.}
Taking $\varphi(u)=-\ln u$ gives the Itakura--Saito divergence
\citep{itakura1968analysis}, for which
$D_{\mathrm{IS}}(e^{q},e^{p}) = e^{q-p}-(q-p)-1$ and
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial L_{\mathrm{IS}}}{\partial p}=1-e^{q-p}.
  \label{eq:is-grad}
\end{equation}
The weight decays fast enough to cancel the residual entirely on the
over-prediction side: as $p\to+\infty$ the gradient saturates at $+1$, a
bounded, correctly-signed step. On the under-prediction side it now
\emph{explodes} like $-e^{q-p}$. Itakura--Saito therefore does not remove
squared error's asymmetry so much as \emph{invert} it, and that is precisely
why it helps: it converts the unrecoverable failure (a gradient that has
decayed to zero) into the recoverable one (a gradient that is too large, which
clipping fixes). It is moreover \emph{scale-invariant}
($D_{\mathrm{IS}}(cT,cu)=D_{\mathrm{IS}}(T,u)$, equivalently \eqref{eq:is-grad}
depends on $q-p$ alone) and is the negative log-likelihood of a
gamma/exponential observation model \citep{fevotte2009isnmf}, giving a natural
uncertainty interpretation for $\Vf$. A useful midpoint, but still one-sided
(Fig.~\ref{fig:loss_curves}, middle).

\paragraph{Why not simply retune $\beta$?}
Squared error and Itakura--Saito are the $\beta=2$ and $\beta=0$ members of the
$\beta$-divergence family \citep{fevotte2011beta,cichocki2010families}, whose
weight is the power law $w(u)\propto u^{\beta-1}$. It is natural to ask whether
some intermediate $\beta$ already does what we want. With $w(u)=u^{\beta-1}$
the gradient is $e^{\beta p}-e^{(\beta-1)p+q}$, so as $p\to+\infty$ it grows
like $e^{\beta p}$: exponentially for every $\beta>0$, and flat for $\beta=0$.
Linear growth in $p$ is therefore attainable at no value of $\beta$, and a
power-law weight cannot produce log-linear tails.

We want to be careful about what that does and does not rule out. One member of
the family, $\beta=1$ --- constant weight, $\varphi(u)=u\ln u-u$, the
generalised KL or I-divergence --- has gradient $e^{p}-e^{q}$, which tends to
$-e^{q}$ as $p\to-\infty$: bounded and non-zero. By the criterion of
\S\ref{sec:03_expmse}, that already removes squared error's \emph{fatal}
tail, leaving only a single exponential explosion above, which clipping
handles. Generalised KL is thus a genuine competitor and not a straw arm, and
we include it as such in \S\ref{sec:05_offpolicy_exp}. The claim we make for
the Spence loss is correspondingly narrow: it is the member whose gradient is
linear in $p$ on \emph{both} sides, and so the one that needs no clipping at
all. Whether that buys anything over a clipped competitor is an empirical
question, which is why the ablation includes both.

\subsection{The Spence loss}
\label{subsec:spence}

Rather than pick a potential and inspect its tails, we ask directly what weight
$w$ we want. Writing $u=e^{p}$ and reading off \eqref{eq:weight-grad}, the two
requirements are:
\begin{align}
  p\to+\infty\ (u\to\infty):\quad & w(u)\,u \sim \ln u
    &&\Longrightarrow\quad w(u)\sim \ln u\,/\,u, \label{eq:req-over}\\
  p\to-\infty\ (u\to 0):\quad & -\,w(u)\,e^{q} \sim e^{q}\ln u
    &&\Longrightarrow\quad w(u)\sim -\ln u, \label{eq:req-under}
\end{align}
i.e.\ in both directions the gradient should grow \emph{linearly in the log-space
prediction} $p=\ln u$. The simplest smooth positive function meeting both is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ w(u) \;=\; \frac{\ln u}{u-1}\ }
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \varphi''(u) \;=\; \frac{\ln u}{u\,(u-1)},
  \label{eq:spence-weight}
\end{equation}
which is positive on all of $(0,\infty)$ (both factors change sign at $u=1$)
and extends smoothly through the removable singularity with $w(1)=1$. It is the
reciprocal of the logarithmic mean of $1$ and $u$ \citep{carlson1972logmean}, and at both extremes it lies
between the squared-error and Itakura--Saito weights: the Spence loss
interpolates the two reference points rather than adding a third unrelated
one. The choice is not unique --- \eqref{eq:req-over}--\eqref{eq:req-under} fix
only the asymptotics --- but \eqref{eq:spence-weight} is the member with a
closed-form gradient and a dilogarithm value (App.~\ref{sec:A_spence}).
Substituting it into \eqref{eq:weight-grad} gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial p} \;=\; p\,\frac{e^{p}-e^{q}}{e^{p}-1}\ }
  \label{eq:spence-grad}
\end{equation}
zero iff $p=q$ (the apparent zero at $p=0$ is cancelled by the pole of
$p/(e^{p}-1)\to1$), with population minimizer $\Vf$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:minimizer}.

\paragraph{Both tails linear in the prediction.}
By construction,
\begin{align}
  p\to+\infty:\quad & \frac{\partial L}{\partial p}\to p,
    &&\text{no $e^{2p}$ blow-up,}\\
  p\to-\infty:\quad & \frac{\partial L}{\partial p}\to p\,e^{q},
    &&\text{non-vanishing corrective signal.}
\end{align}
We stress what is and is not being claimed. The gradient is linear in the
\emph{prediction} $p$, not in the error $p-q$, so the loss grows like
$\tfrac12 p^{2}$ for over-predictions and like $\tfrac12 p^{2}e^{q}$ for
under-predictions: it is quadratic in $p$ on both sides, but with
target-dependent curvature on the under-prediction branch. That $e^{q}$ factor
is the reason the loss is not scale-invariant, and it is deliberate --- it
up-weights exactly the rare high-value targets that dominate the log-sum-exp
and are hardest to find (\S\ref{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy}), while still growing
only polynomially in $p$. To our knowledge this is the first Bregman divergence
proposed for exp-space value regression with both tails controlled in this
sense. The alternative strand of work attacks the same conditioning problem by
changing the \emph{objective} rather than the divergence: log-variance and
adjoint losses \citep{nusken2021logvariance,richter2020vargrad,domingoenrich2024adjoint},
and the trajectory-balance family
\citep{malkin2022trajectory,madan2023subtb,venkatraman2024rtb}, of which
\citet{zhang2024dgfs} is closest --- it also learns intermediate
log-partition functions for a diffusion sampler, but through a partial-trajectory
objective rather than by regressing an unbiased exponential-space target. Those
objectives buy conditioning by giving up the unbiased target; we keep the
target and change only the divergence applied to it.

\paragraph{Value via the Spence function.}
The divergence value (used only for monitoring; training uses
\eqref{eq:spence-grad}) is expressible through the Spence function
$S(x)=\Spence(1-e^{x})$ \citep{lewin1981dilogarithm}, using the dilogarithm identity
$S'(x)=-\,x e^{x}/(e^{x}-1)=x/\mathrm{expm1}(-x)$; hence the name. The full
derivation and the numerically stable branch-wise gradient --- finite for all
float inputs, unlike naive autograd, which returns NaN once the \emph{target}
reaches $q\gtrsim85$ nats in single precision --- are in App.~\ref{sec:A_spence}.

\paragraph{Implied observation model.}
By the duality between regular Bregman divergences and regular exponential
families \citep{banerjee2005clustering,azoury2001relative}, minimizing any $\Breg$ is maximum
likelihood under the exponential family whose cumulant is the convex conjugate
$\varphi^{*}$; squared error recovers a Gaussian and Itakura--Saito a gamma.
For \eqref{eq:spence-weight} the conjugate is not a standard named family, so
we report the correspondence rather than a closed-form likelihood; the
practical content of the weight is the tail behavior above.

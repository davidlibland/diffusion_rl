\section{Signal sparsity and on-policy sampling}
\label{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy}

A better-conditioned loss fixes what happens once a high-value target is in
the batch. It does nothing about how rarely one arrives. This section is about
the second problem, which is what limits value learning in high dimension.

\subsection{Why off-policy anchors run out}

The value is a log-sum-exp, so it is a soft maximum: the expectation
$\E_{\Base}[e^{r(X_1)}]$ is dominated by whatever region of the base marginal
carries the largest reward, and the size of that region shrinks as the tilt
grows. The cost is quantitative rather than heuristic. Estimating $\E[e^{r}]$
by importance sampling from $\mathbb{P}$ requires on the order of
$\exp\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\star}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})$ samples
\citep{chatterjee2018sample}, and that KL is exactly the reward tilt the task
asks for. Off-policy anchors draw $x_1$ from the base marginal $\nu$ with no
reference to $r$ (\S\ref{subsec:targets}), so their useful-target rate decays
on that schedule. Every anchor still carries a valid target; almost all of them
carry an uninformative one.

\begin{remark}[Our benchmark holds this cost fixed, by construction]
\label{rem:kl-fixed}
The constant-headroom calibration of \S\ref{subsec:design} bounds the very
quantity this argument turns on. Writing the headroom as
$\E_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}[r]-\E_{\Base}[r]=6$ nats and using
$\log Z=\max_u\{\E[r]-\mathrm{KL}\}$, one gets
$\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\star}\|\mathbb{P})
 =\E_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}[r]-\log Z\le 6$, and measured on our instances it
is $1.4$--$2.6$ nats and \emph{flat} in $d$ ($1.36$, $2.10$, $2.55$, $2.60$,
$1.83$ at $d=2,8,32,128,512$). The importance-sampling cost is therefore
bounded and roughly dimension-independent here. That is the design working as
intended --- dimension is the only factor varying --- but it means the study
tests on-policy sampling \emph{at} fixed sparsity rather than testing how the
benefit scales \emph{with} sparsity. We return to this in
\S\ref{sec:07_experiments} and \S\ref{sec:09_conclusion}.
\end{remark}

The way out is already available. By \S\ref{sec:02_setting}(ii) the
H-martingale identity survives \emph{any} adapted twist once the Girsanov ratio
\eqref{eq:girsanov-rho} is applied, so the sampler may be chosen freely to put
mass where the integrand is large without changing the object being learned.
Since the optimal twist is $2a\nabla \Vf$ and $\Vf$ is what we are fitting, the
learner supplies its own proposal --- a self-consistent loop of the same shape
as controlled SMC \citep{heng2020csmc}.

\subsection{Coverage beats concentration}

The obvious move is to sharpen the twist and concentrate on the reward
corridor. In our dimensional-scaling study that is consistently the wrong move.
Twisting with the \emph{analytic} value --- the strongest twist available, and
an oracle no method could actually learn --- makes matters worse at $d=512$,
and splicing off-policy coverage back alongside it does not rescue the arm
(App.~\ref{subsec:b-failures}). The failure is not one of tuning: introducing
the concentration gradually rather than at once leaves the outcome unchanged
across two orders of magnitude of ramp speed.

The reading we take is that in high dimension a concentrated sampler buys
target quality at a ruinous price in coverage. The corridor is a thin set; a
value network trained only along it is unconstrained everywhere else, and the
guided rollouts that follow immediately leave the region where it was fitted.

\subsection{The method we recommend}

Accordingly the useful lever is not a sharper twist but a \emph{wider} one. We
keep the mild twist the learner produces on its own and add coverage in the
place the corridor is thinnest, using two modifications.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Backward-noising expansion.} Each sampled point $(x_s,s)$ is
  noised backwards through the base backward kernel
  \eqref{eq:backward-kernel} and the target is attached to the noised point as
  well, converting one trajectory into many training pairs at earlier, noisier
  times. The kernel is independent of the terminal law
  (App.~\ref{sec:D_proofs}), so the construction is available for any
  pinned-interpolant base model, not only for Brownian motion.

  One caveat needs stating precisely, because it is easy to overstate this
  step. The identity \eqref{eq:hmart} transfers the target exactly when the
  point being noised is \emph{base}-distributed, as it is for the off-policy
  anchors of \S\ref{subsec:targets}. Under a twisted sweep it is not:
  $x_s\sim\mu\neq p_{\Base}^{(s)}$, so the conditional law of $x_s$ given the
  noised $x_t$ is $\mu(x_s)p_{\Base}(x_t\mid x_s)$ renormalised, which is not
  the base conditional, and the Girsanov weight of \eqref{eq:girsanov-rho}
  does not repair it --- that weight corrects the forward sweep, not the
  reversal. Carrying the sweep's accumulated ratio $\rho_{0\to s}$ as a row
  weight restores exactness; the arms reported here do not, so the expanded
  rows are biased under the twist. We report this as an empirically useful
  approximation rather than an exact identity, which is what the ablations in
  App.~\ref{sec:B_onpolicy_zoo} actually establish.
\item \textbf{Finer integration.} A larger Euler--Maruyama step count for the
  sweep. The two are independent --- one changes where data sits, the other
  changes sampling bias --- and their gains are close to additive.
\end{itemize}

Neither is a target-network effect in disguise. Both modifications multiply the
rows produced per trajectory and so slow the dataset's regeneration by up to
$6\times$, which would be an obvious confound. Subsampling each epoch back to
the original cadence \emph{after} generation leaves every gain intact
(App.~\ref{sec:B_onpolicy_zoo}); the effect is where the data sits, not how
slowly it moves.

\subsection{Result}

Fitting the expansion fraction and step count as part of the per-dimension
hyperparameter laws, rather than hand-setting them, yields a single
configuration family that is significantly above off-policy training at
$d=8$--$32$ and never significantly below it anywhere in $2\le d\le512$
(\S\ref{sec:07_experiments}, Fig.~\ref{fig:dimscaling}). The fitted laws select
nonzero expansion at every dimension.

Two scope conditions. The recipe was tuned on this benchmark family, and it
does not transfer unchanged to the Boltzmann targets of
\S\ref{sec:07_experiments}; and all of our rewards are cheap to evaluate, so we
count samples rather than reward calls (\S\ref{sec:09_conclusion}). The zoo of
alternative on-policy methods, the mechanisms that failed, and the per-method
ablations are in App.~\ref{sec:B_onpolicy_zoo}.

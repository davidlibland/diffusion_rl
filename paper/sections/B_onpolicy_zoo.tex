\section{On-policy sampling: the method zoo, the levers, and what failed}
\label{sec:B_onpolicy_zoo}

\S\ref{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy} reports the single on-policy configuration we
recommend. This appendix records how we arrived at it, including the arms that did not
work --- several of which are mechanisms the literature would predict should
help.

Unless stated otherwise, numbers are \emph{frac.\ closed} --- the fraction of
the calibrated $6$-nat headroom closed at the plateau (\S\ref{sec:07_experiments})
--- at $d=512$, on paired nested seeds, with $\pm$ one standard error.

\subsection{How on-policy targets are formed}
\label{subsec:b-targets}
\S\ref{subsec:targets} states these in outline; here they are in full.

\paragraph{On-policy (bootstrapped) targets.}
Roll a sequential-Monte-Carlo sweep forward from $X_0=0$ under the current
twist $u=2a\nabla \Vf_\theta$. A particle at $(x_t,t)$ spawns $k$ children at
$s=t+\Delta t$ under the guided kernel; writing $\rho_i$ for the per-step ratio
of \eqref{eq:girsanov-rho} and $v_i = \Vf_\theta(x^{(i)}_s, s)$, the target
attached to the parent is the log-mean-exp over its siblings,
\begin{equation}
  q \;=\; \log\Big(\tfrac{1}{k}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\rho_i\,e^{v_i}\Big).
  \label{eq:bootstrap-target}
\end{equation}
It is worth being exact about what this estimates. Define the one-step backup
operator $\mathcal{T}_s$ --- the continuous-time analogue of the soft Bellman
backup \citep{haarnoja2017softq} --- acting on a candidate value function $W$ by
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal{T}_s W)(x,t) \;=\; \log\E_{\Base}\!\big[\,e^{W(X_s,s)}\,\big|\,X_t=x\,\big].
  \label{eq:backup-op}
\end{equation}
The children are i.i.d.\ from the guided kernel given the parent, so by
\eqref{eq:girsanov}
\begin{equation}
  \E\big[e^{q}\mid x_t\big] \;=\; e^{(\mathcal{T}_s \Vf_\theta)(x_t,t)}.
  \label{eq:backup-unbiased}
\end{equation}
That is the whole guarantee: \eqref{eq:bootstrap-target} is an exp-space
unbiased estimate of the backup of \emph{the current network}, and of nothing
else. In particular it is not an estimate of $\Vf(x_t,t)$. Because
$e^{\Vf_\theta}$ is not harmonic, the children's values need not be good
estimates of the true value at the parent, and whatever error $\Vf_\theta$
carries at time $s$ is inherited rather than corrected. What keeps the
recursion honest is the terminal step, where the children carry $q=r(X_1)$
exactly; the scheme is useful to the extent that this anchoring propagates
backwards. By \eqref{eq:hmart} the true value is a fixed point,
$\mathcal{T}_s\Vf=\Vf$, but we prove nothing about convergence to it and treat
the bootstrap as an empirical device throughout.

\paragraph{Backward-noising expansion.}
Because $e^{\Vf}$ is harmonic, a target attached at $(x_s,s)$ remains valid at
any point noised \emph{backwards} from it under the base backward kernel. For
the pinned interpolants this kernel is available in closed form and, notably,
does not depend on the terminal law $\nu$ at all:
\begin{equation}
  X_t \,\big|\, X_s = x_s \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\Big(\tfrac{t}{s}\,x_s,\;
    2a\,\tfrac{t(s-t)}{s}\,I\Big),
  \qquad t<s,
  \label{eq:backward-kernel}
\end{equation}
because conditioning a Brownian bridge on an interior point leaves a Brownian
bridge to that point, whatever the endpoint distribution. The expansion is
therefore available for base models with nontrivial drift, not only for
Brownian motion. The hypothesis that does the work here is about the
\emph{marginal}, not the kernel: \emph{provided} $x_s$ is drawn from the base
marginal $p_{\Base}^{(\cdot,s)}$ and $x_t$ from the backward kernel, the pair
$(x_t,q)$ carries the base joint law and \eqref{eq:hmart} applies unchanged.
That holds for the off-policy anchors above; \S\ref{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy}
returns to what happens when the points instead come from a twisted sweep, for
which it does not. This turns one trajectory into many
training pairs at earlier, noisier times, and is the mechanism behind our best
on-policy variant (\S\ref{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy}).

\subsection{The zoo}

All on-policy arms run a twisted SMC sweep and differ in how targets are
formed from it.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{ssmc} (single-seed MC): the log-mean-exp over siblings of
  \eqref{eq:bootstrap-target}, with Monte-Carlo terminal targets.
\item \textbf{ssmc-td}: the same sweep with TD($\lambda$) targets, so the
  regression target bootstraps through a $\lambda$-weighted mixture.
\item \textbf{amctl} (ancestral MC + TD($\lambda$)): resampling ancestrally
  rather than per-step.
\item \textbf{fbrrt} / \textbf{fbrrt\_cv}: forward-backward
  reverse-ratio-tracking variants that add their own drift modification, with
  \texttt{\_cv} using an EMA shadow network as the policy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two levers that worked, and they stack}

\paragraph{Backward-noising expansion.}
Reuse a sampled trajectory by noising each of its points backwards through
\eqref{eq:backward-kernel} and attaching the same target. This adds training
pairs at earlier, noisier times, precisely where an on-policy corridor is
thinnest. At $d=512$ it gains $+3.0$ over the ssmc control and reaches a
statistical tie with off-policy ($-1.8\pm1.2$, $p=0.18$).

\paragraph{Finer integration.}
Raising the Euler--Maruyama step count from the law-fitted $\approx19$ to $60$
gains $+3.2$. The fitted law was wrong at high dimension for a mundane reason:
the anchor sweeps ran at $d\le128$, where the integrator bias is invisible.

\paragraph{They stack.}
Together (\texttt{expand\_ns60}) they gain $+7.0\pm2.5$ over the ssmc control,
close to the sum of the parts, confirming independent mechanisms --- one acts
on where data sits, the other on sampling bias. This was the first on-policy
configuration to land above off-policy at $d=512$, and at the full $30$-seed
grid it holds: $37.1\pm2.7$ against off-policy's $34.3\pm2.6$, a paired
$+2.8\pm1.4$.

\subsection{The control that mattered: staleness}

Both levers multiply the number of rows produced per trajectory, so the
dataset regenerates up to $6\times$ less often ($\approx1{,}920$ gradient steps
between regenerations versus $\approx304$ for the control). Slow-moving data is
an implicit target network, and could by itself explain the gain. The
\texttt{\_sub} arms subsample each epoch uniformly back to the control's row
count \emph{after} generation, restoring the original cadence exactly.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
arm & mean & vs.\ ssmc control & vs.\ its full arm \\
\midrule
\texttt{expand\_ns60\_sub} & $34.7\pm3.5$ & $+8.9\pm1.4$ ($p<.001$, 10/10) & $+1.9\pm2.6$ ($p=.48$) \\
\texttt{expand\_sub}       & $28.7\pm1.6$ & $+2.8\pm2.3$ & $-0.2\pm1.5$ ($p=.91$) \\
\texttt{ns60\_sub}         & $27.9\pm1.9$ & $+2.0\pm1.5$ & $-1.2\pm1.4$ ($p=.39$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Staleness explains none of the gains: every \texttt{\_sub} arm matches its
full-epoch counterpart within noise. The controls also separate the
mechanisms. \texttt{ns60\_sub} keeps the integrator gain while discarding two
thirds of its rows, so that lever lives in the sampling and not the row count;
\texttt{expand\_sub} shows the expanded off-path rows are worth at least as
much as the on-path rows they displace. These arms consume the same training
rows as everything else while \emph{discarding} most of what they generate, and
still win.

\subsection{Generalisation across methods, and one clean split}

Applying the recipe to the other on-policy methods at $d=512$ (paired seeds
0--9, off-policy control $30.7$):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{llcc}
\toprule
method & arm & mean & vs.\ own control \\
\midrule
ssmc-td & \texttt{expand\_ns60}     & $32.1\pm2.0$ & $+6.5\pm2.0$ ($p=.011$) \\
ssmc-td & \texttt{expand\_ns60\_sub} & $27.4\pm1.9$ & $+1.8\pm1.3$ ($p=.19$) \\
amctl   & \texttt{expand\_ns60}     & $27.8\pm2.5$ & $+8.0\pm1.6$ ($p=.001$, 10/10) \\
amctl   & \texttt{expand\_ns60\_sub} & $28.5\pm1.8$ & $+8.6\pm1.3$ ($p<.001$, 10/10) \\
fbrrt   & \texttt{expand\_ns60}     & $13.6\pm3.8$ & $+3.1\pm6.2$ ($p=.63$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The recipe transfers to both SMC-TD methods, and the staleness control exposes
a mechanistic split worth recording: ssmc and amctl, whose targets are pure
Monte-Carlo, keep their full gain under \texttt{\_sub}, while ssmc-td --- the
one method whose targets bootstrap --- loses most of its gain when the cadence
is restored ($+6.5\to+1.8$). Bootstrapped targets genuinely benefit from a
slower-moving dataset, on top of the placement effect. fbrrt does not benefit
reliably, and its high-dimensional deficit is not recipe-shaped.

\subsection{Folding the levers into the hyperparameter laws}

Treating expansion fraction, step count and epoch size as tunable and refitting
the per-dimension laws (with an added $d=512$ anchor) yields one configuration
family that is significantly above off-policy at $d=8$--$32$ (up to $+13.4$
paired) and never significantly below it anywhere in $2\le d\le512$. As in
\S\ref{sec:07_experiments} we do not claim equivalence at the remaining
dimensions: those rest on failure to reject, and we ran no equivalence test. The fitted laws chose \emph{nonzero
expansion at every dimension} and a moderate constant step count ($29$--$33$,
not the hand-set $60$).

The fixed recipe, by contrast, cost up to $-15$ points at low dimension. The
reason is instructive: it grafted \texttt{frac}$=1.0$ and \texttt{n\_steps}$=60$
onto hyperparameters fitted under the old configuration. Moderate expansion
with co-adapted learning rate and twist is harmless at $d=2$--$8$; the damage
came from un-co-adapted knobs, not from expansion itself.

\subsection{What failed}
\label{subsec:b-failures}

\paragraph{Concentrating on the reward corridor (oracle twist).}
Twisting the sampler toward high reward using the \emph{analytic} value ---
the strongest possible twist --- makes things worse at $d=512$, and splicing
off-policy coverage back in does not rescue it: the blended arm sits $3.7$
below the plain ssmc control and $8.5$ below off-policy. Unweighting the
corridor sources recovers only about half the damage. On this benchmark family at $d=512$, broad coverage of the marginal beat
concentration on the corridor in every arm we ran, which is why the
recommended method expands coverage rather than sharpening the twist.

\paragraph{Trust-region control of the twist.}
Implementing the KL-budgeted twist update of \citet{wang2026tritsmc} left the
twist untouched: the mixing coefficient hit $\beta=1.0$ at all $\approx50$
updates even at the tightest budget, so every budget produced bit-identical
runs. The learned twist already moves far slower than any reasonable KL
budget. This null retroactively explains why EMA and staleness knobs barely
move ssmc --- its twist is not the fast-moving object; for ssmc-td the
bootstrapped targets are.

\paragraph{Ramped concentration.}
Introducing the oracle twist gradually rather than at once does not help. Ramp
speed was varied over two orders of magnitude and had no effect
($20.1/19.2/20.9$ across the scan; a slow ramp spanning most of training gives
$20.2\pm1.4$, statistically identical to the instant ramp at $p=.75$).
Concentration harm is ramp-speed invariant in every reachable regime.

\paragraph{Both extremes of importance correction.}
Within the expansion family the weighting scheme traces a clean bias--variance
U: uniform weights (no correction, biased targets) give $18.0\pm1.7$; raw
weights clamped at $100\times$ (unbiased, high variance) give $21.9\pm2.7$; the
dual-tempered weights of \citet{wang2026tritsmc} give $25.2\pm3.4$. The
tempered form is the one mechanism from the trust-region literature we adopt.

\paragraph{Known-open.}
\texttt{fbrrt\_cv} returns negative frac.\ closed at $d\ge128$, i.e.\ it ends
below the base process; we have not audited this and make no claim about the
variant. The hidden width is capped at $256$ for all $d\ge8$, so
capacity--dimension interaction is unexplored, and the $6$-nat headroom is a
single difficulty point --- the crossover dimension between on- and off-policy
very likely moves with it.

\begin{remark}[The benchmark holds the sampling cost fixed]
\label{rem:kl-fixed}
The motivation for on-policy sampling is that importance sampling for
$\E[e^{r}]$ costs on the order of
$e^{\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\star}\|\mathbb{P})}$ samples. The
constant-headroom calibration of \S\ref{sec:07_experiments} bounds exactly
that quantity: with
$\E_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}[r]-\E_{\Base}[r]=6$ nats and
$\log Z\ge\E_{\Base}[r]$ by Jensen,
$\mathrm{KL}=\E_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}[r]-\log Z\le6$; measured on our
instances it is $1.4$--$2.6$ nats and flat in $d$ ($1.36$, $2.10$, $2.55$,
$2.60$, $1.83$ at $d=2,8,32,128,512$). The study therefore probes on-policy
sampling \emph{at} fixed sparsity and cannot measure how the benefit scales
\emph{with} sparsity. Together with \S\ref{subsec:interaction} this is why we
do not present the comparison as a result about samplers.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The sampler comparison across dimension}
\label{subsec:b-dimscaling}
This is the study that motivated the tool of \S\ref{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy}.
We report it here rather than in the body because
\S\ref{subsec:interaction} shows its headline is substantially an artifact of
the loss it was measured with: the same comparison under a better-conditioned
loss gives roughly a third of the effect. It remains a fair record of what the
sampler does \emph{at a fixed loss}, and of how the configuration was arrived
at.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/dimscaling}
  \caption{Headroom closed against dimension, $n=30$ paired seeds per point,
    error bars $\pm1$ s.e. \textbf{Left:} absolute performance. \textbf{Right:}
    paired difference against the off-policy control on the same instances;
    stars mark $p<0.05$. Fitting the recipe knobs (expansion fraction and
    integration steps) as part of the per-dimension hyperparameter laws removes
    the high-dimensional collapse: the dotted family loses $7.8$ points to
    off-policy at $d=512$, while the solid one is significantly above the
    control at $d=8$--$32$ and not significantly below it at any dimension.
    Stars are uncorrected for multiple comparisons.}
  \label{fig:dimscaling}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:dimscaling} shows the result that motivates
\S\ref{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy}. On-policy sampling is a large win at moderate
dimension --- for the Monte-Carlo-target method, $+11.2$ points at $d=8$,
$+4.5$ at $d=16$ and $+2.4$ at $d=32$, all $p<0.05$; the TD($\lambda$) variant
reaches $+13.4$ at $d=8$ --- and the advantage decays as $d$ grows.

That decay deserves comment, because it runs opposite to the naive reading of
\S\ref{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy}: if on-policy sampling exists to fight signal
sparsity, and sparsity worsens with dimension, the advantage should grow. It
does not, and the reason is the benchmark's own design. The constant-headroom
calibration holds $\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\star}\|\mathbb{P})$ at $1.4$--$2.6$
nats at \emph{every} dimension, so the importance-sampling cost this method
addresses does not in fact grow along this axis. What does grow is the volume
the value network must represent, a capacity and coverage problem rather than a
sampling one --- consistent with concentration becoming actively harmful at
$d=512$ (App.~\ref{subsec:b-failures}). We therefore do not read
Fig.~\ref{fig:dimscaling} as evidence about how the sparsity benefit scales
with sparsity; it is not designed to measure that.

The interesting part is the failure mode of the naive version. Hyperparameter
laws fitted without the expansion and step-count knobs (dotted) track
off-policy until $d=64$ and then fall away, reaching $-4.5$ at $d=256$ and
$-7.8$ at $d=512$: on-policy sampling becomes actively harmful in exactly the
regime where its motivation --- signal sparsity --- is strongest. Admitting the
two coverage knobs into the laws (solid) removes the collapse without
hand-switching between configurations by dimension. The refitted family is
significantly above the control at $d=8$--$32$ and never significantly below
it anywhere. We avoid the word ``matches'' for the remaining dimensions: those
rest on failure to reject at $n=30$, and with paired standard errors near
$1.4$ points the data cannot exclude a deficit of a few points. Establishing
equivalence would require a test against a pre-specified margin, which we did
not run, and the stars in Fig.~\ref{fig:dimscaling} are uncorrected for
testing across ten dimensions and two arms.

The refitted family gives up about $3$ points to the un-refitted one at $d\le16$, where both are far above off-policy anyway,
and recovers $6$ to $9$ points at $d\ge256$, where the un-refitted one is below
it. Per-dimension retuning would likely recover both, at the cost of the
single-family property this study was built to test.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:01_intro}

Steering a diffusion model with a reward --- fine-tuning it toward a preference
model, conditioning it on a constraint, or using it to sample a Boltzmann
density --- has a single mathematical formulation, given by
\citet{uehara2024finetuning} following the entropy-regularized control
tradition of \citet{daipra1991stochastic} and \citet{kappen2005path}. One asks
for the path measure $\mathbb{P}^{\star}$ that reweights a base diffusion
$\mathbb{P}$ by $e^{r(X_1)}$, and the object that produces it is the soft value
function
\begin{equation}
  \Vf(x,t) \;=\; \log\E_{\Base}\!\big[\,e^{r(X_1)}\mid X_t=x\,\big],
  \label{eq:intro-value}
\end{equation}
whose gradient is the optimal control, $u^{\star}=2a\nabla \Vf$. Learning $\Vf$
is therefore the whole problem, and $\Vf$ is a conditional log-partition
function: at $t=0$ it is $\log Z$ itself.

Log-partition functions are awkward to regress. The H-martingale property
$e^{\Vf(x,t)}=\E_{\Base}[e^{\Vf(X_s,s)}\mid X_t=x]$ supplies targets that are
conditionally unbiased --- but for $e^{\Vf}$, not for $\Vf$, and no plug-in in
log space repairs the difference, since the Jensen gap that separates
$\E[q]$ from $\log\E[e^{q}]$ is precisely the quantity a soft maximum is made
of. The target is therefore stuck in exponential space, and the obvious loss,
squared error between $e^{p}$ and $e^{q}$, behaves badly there. Its gradient is
$2e^{p}(e^{p}-e^{q})$, which explodes as $e^{2p}$ when the model over-predicts
and, more seriously, decays to zero when it under-predicts. The second case is
the one that matters: it is exactly the state whose true value is large and
whose predicted value is small --- a rare, high-reward target the model has
failed to find --- and gradient clipping, which tames the first pathology,
cannot manufacture a gradient that has already vanished.

Our starting observation is that this loss was never forced on us. For any
strictly convex $\varphi$, the Bregman divergence $\Breg(e^{q},e^{p})$ has the
same population minimizer, $\Vf$ --- the Savage representation of proper
scoring rules \citep{savage1971elicitation,gneiting2007proper,banerjee2005clustering}
--- so $\varphi$ is free to be chosen for conditioning. What makes that freedom
usable is that in log space the whole family collapses:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial p}\,\Breg\big(e^{q},e^{p}\big)
  \;=\; w(e^{p})\,\big(e^{p}-e^{q}\big),
  \qquad w(u)=u\,\varphi''(u)>0 .
  \label{eq:intro-weight}
\end{equation}
A Bregman loss reaches the optimizer only through the scalar weight $w$, so
designing one means choosing a positive function on $(0,\infty)$ and nothing
else. Squared error is $w(u)=2u$, growing with the prediction, which is why it
explodes above and dies below; Itakura--Saito is $w(u)=1/u$, which inverts the
asymmetry rather than removing it. Both are power laws, and no power law will
do: with $w(u)\propto u^{\beta-1}$ the gradient grows like $e^{\beta p}$, so it
is exponential for every $\beta>0$ and flat at $\beta=0$.

Solving \eqref{eq:intro-weight} for a weight whose gradient grows linearly in
$p$ in both directions gives $w(u)=\ln u/(u-1)$, the reciprocal logarithmic
mean, and with it a loss we call the \emph{Spence loss}: its gradient is
$p\,(e^{p}-e^{q})/(e^{p}-1)$ in closed form, and its value is expressible
through the Spence function $\Spence(1-e^{x})$, which names it.

A better-conditioned loss does not, however, make rare targets easier to find.
That is a sampling problem, and in high dimension it dominates: importance
sampling for $\E[e^{r}]$ from the base process needs on the order of
$e^{\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\star}\|\mathbb{P})}$ samples
\citep{chatterjee2018sample}. Because the H-martingale identity survives any
adapted twist once the Girsanov ratio is applied, the sampler is free, and the
learned value is itself the optimal twist. The second half of this paper
studies that loop, where the useful finding is a negative one: at high
dimension, concentrating the sampler on the reward corridor --- even with an
oracle twist --- is worse than not doing so, and what helps instead is widening
coverage at small $t$.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item We show that a Bregman loss applied to the unbiased exponential-space
  target acts on the optimizer only through the gradient weight
  $w(u)=u\varphi''(u)$ of \eqref{eq:intro-weight}, which reduces loss design
  for value functions to the choice of one positive function, and places
  squared error and Itakura--Saito as the $\beta=2$ and $\beta=0$ members of a
  power-law family none of whose members has controlled tails
  (\S\ref{sec:04_bregman}).
\item We derive the Spence loss by solving for the weight
  $w(u)=\ln u/(u-1)$ whose gradient is linear in the log-space prediction in
  both tails, give its closed-form gradient and dilogarithm value, and give a
  branch-wise evaluation that is finite for every finite float input, unlike
  automatic differentiation of the divergence
  (\S\ref{subsec:spence}, App.~\ref{sec:A_spence}).
\item We measure five losses --- Spence, exponential-space squared error,
  Itakura--Saito with and without gradient clipping, and the biased log-space
  baseline --- against each other with per-loss learning-rate tuning on a
  benchmark whose value function is available analytically, scoring control
  quality, the RMSE of the learned value against the exact one, and stability
  (\S\ref{sec:05_offpolicy_exp}).
\item We show that on-policy twisted sampling, with coverage widened by
  backward noising, matches or beats off-policy training at every dimension
  from $2$ to $512$, and that the naive version instead collapses to $7.8$
  points below off-policy at $d=512$
  (\S\ref{sec:06_sparsity_onpolicy}, \S\ref{sec:07_experiments}).
\item On GMM-40 the learned value recovers $\log Z$ to $0.08$ nats with
  complete mode coverage, ahead of the published samplers we compare against
  on that target (\S\ref{subsec:gmm40}).
\end{itemize}
